About this calculator

Average velocity in a pipe comes straight from the continuity equation (Eq. 8.5), and velocity head from Eq. 8.1 — both direct physics, not a lookup table. The textbook's Figure 8.3 is a nomograph for reading velocity head off a chart; this tool computes it directly and adds the practical question the chapter raises in the text: is this velocity high enough to risk water hammer?

hv = Vm²/2g, g = 32.2 ft/s²

The chart shows a straight line for every standard pipe size in Table 8.5 (velocity is directly proportional to flow rate for a fixed diameter), with your selected size highlighted in gold. The shaded band marks velocities above your chosen threshold.

Cross-check with Example 8.2: that example uses a 4-in pipe with d=4 in exactly (switch to custom diameter mode to match it) and gets 10.2 ft/s — more than double the 5 ft/s guideline. Pick the standard "4-in nominal aluminum sprinkler" size instead (actual inside diameter 3.9 in, Table 8.5) and the real velocity is slightly higher still, about 10.7 ft/s — the textbook's own worked example is, by this check, a water-hammer risk either way. Switching to the 10-in pipe used in Example 8.3 drops that to about 1.6 ft/s.
